# The Mom Test — Decoded

Nov 21, 2026 · @kontey

The Mom Test teaches one skill: ask about people's real past behaviour, never their opinion of your idea, because everyone (even your mom) will lie to protect your feelings. Scripts for YouTube, Reels, carousel and LinkedIn are in the other files in this folder.

## 1. Diagnosis: what you actually uploaded

Your PDF is not the book. It is a 105-page **Bookey summary** of Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*: chapter summaries, a quote list, Q&A and a quiz. Good enough to teach from, with three gaps.

- **Bookey's own add-ons.** The "Critical Thinking" boxes (Blue Ocean Strategy, Lean Startup comparisons) are Bookey's opinions, not Rob's. Don't credit them to the book on camera.
- **No real stories.** The original is full of founder stories; the summary keeps only two (the cookbook app, the powdered condiment). Your content needs your own examples to land.

**The one big idea, as an analogy:** asking people "Is my idea good?" is like asking a doctor's waiting room "Do I look sick?" Everyone says you look fine to be polite. A real doctor ignores what you say you feel and checks your blood. The Mom Test is the blood test: you check what people *did*, not what they *say*.

**Who it's for:** anyone who needs truth from other people before spending time or money. Founders validating ideas, freelancers finding clients, creators testing content, students picking projects.

**The arc of the book (9 chapters, 3 phases):**

1. **Ask right** (Ch 1–3): the rules, the bad data to ignore, and the scary questions that matter.
2. **Run the conversation** (Ch 4–5): keep it casual, then push for real commitment.
3. **Run the system** (Ch 6–9): find people to talk to, pick a narrow customer, and make learning a team process.

## 2. The 3 rules + bad vs good questions

A question passes the Mom Test when even someone who loves you can't lie in the answer. Three rules get you there:

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you pitch, they start protecting your feelings.
2. **Ask about specific past events, not opinions or the future.** "Last time" beats "would you ever".
3. **Talk less, listen more.** If you're talking more than them, you're pitching, not learning.

**Why it works:** people are bad at predicting what they'll do and good at remembering what they did. Past behaviour is data. Future promises are guesses dressed as data.

| Bad question (fails the test) | Why it fails | Good question (passes) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do you think it's a good idea? | Only you know your idea; they'll be polite | How do you deal with this right now? |
| Would you buy a product that does X? | Future promise, always "yes" | When did you last spend money fixing this? |
| How much would you pay for X? | Hypothetical, lowball or fake-high | What are you paying for it today? |
| Would you use an app for this? | Asks them to imagine | Walk me through the last time this happened. |
| What would your dream product do? | Customers own problems, not solutions | What have you already tried? Why didn't it work? |
| Do you ever struggle with X? | Leading; invites a fake yes | What's the hardest part of \[their task\]? |

**The golden follow-ups** (use in every conversation):

- "Why do you bother?" — finds the motivation behind an action.
- "What happens if you don't solve it?" — tells you if the problem is big or a mild annoyance.
- "What else have you tried?" — no attempts means the pain isn't real.
- "Who else should I talk to?" — turns one conversation into three.

## 4. The full toolkit

Every framework from the book, ready to use.

**A. The 3 types of bad data and how to fix each**

| Bad data | Sounds like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compliments | "Love it!", "That's so cool" | Ignore it. Ask: "How are you handling this today?" |
| Fluff | "I usually…", "I would…", "I'd definitely…" | Anchor it: "When was the last time? Walk me through it." |
| Ideas / feature requests | "You should add X" | Dig: "Why do you want that? What would it let you do?" |

**B. The 3 currencies of commitment** (the more they give, the more you can trust their words)

- **Time:** a follow-up meeting with a clear agenda, using a trial, giving feedback on a prototype.
- **Reputation:** an intro to their boss, a peer or a decision-maker; a public testimonial.
- **Money:** a pre-order, deposit, letter of intent, a paid pilot.

**C. Zombie lead warning signs:** they keep saying nice things, meetings end with "keep me posted", and there's never a next step. Fix it by asking for a concrete commitment so they get a real chance to say no.

**D. Earlyvangelists:** your first customers look slightly irrational. They have the problem, know they have it, have money for it, and have already hacked together their own fix. If someone isn't emotional about the problem, they won't be first to buy.

**E. Customer slicing → a who-where pair.** Keep asking "which subset of them wants this most?" until you can say *who* and *where you'll find them*. Example: "small businesses" → "real estate brokers" → "brokers in Pune selling resale flats who post their own Instagram listings". Now you know where to look.

**F. Finding conversations**

- Cold outreach: low reply rate, but a few talks are enough to start. Its only job is to lead to warm intros.
- Warm intros: ask friends, mentors, teachers, past clients. Ask twice and you can reach almost anyone.
- Bring them to you: run a meetup, teach, post, write. Expertise draws conversations in.
- Find a good excuse: a shared interest, a research project, a student status.
- The intro formula: **Vision** (what you're trying to fix) → **Framing** (what stage you're at, no product to sell) → **Weakness** (the specific thing you're stuck on) → **Pedestal** (why *they* can help) → **Ask** (a short, specific request).

**G. The process loop**

1. **Prep:** your 3 big questions, basic research on the person, the scary question you'll ask.
2. **Talk:** casual, short, mostly listening; one person talks, one takes notes.
3. **Review:** same day, share exact quotes, update what you believe, update your 3 questions.
4. Repeat until you stop hearing anything new.

**H. Two thought experiments to find important questions**

- "If this fails, what's the most likely reason?"
- "What would have to be true for this to be a huge success?"

**I. Market risk vs product risk:** market risk is "will they want it and pay?" (conversations answer this). Product risk is "can I build it?" (only building answers this). Don't ask customers to solve product risk.

## 5. Critical take

The core method is solid and widely used; its blind spots show up at scale and with brand-new behaviours.

**Established (what the book argues and practitioners broadly agree with):**

- Opinions about the future are unreliable; past behaviour and money spent are better evidence.
- Pitching early contaminates the feedback.
- A narrow first customer segment produces clearer signal than "everyone".

**Where it's weaker (my reading, not settled fact):**

- **Brand-new behaviours.** If nobody has done the thing before (a new AI workflow, say), there's no past behaviour to ask about. You'll need prototypes and pre-sales sooner.
- **Volume businesses.** For a cheap consumer app, 20 chats won't tell you much. Pair conversations with landing-page tests and real usage data.
- **Introverts and non-native speakers.** "Keep it casual" is easy advice for confident talkers. A short written set of questions you can glance at is fine.
- **Process overhead.** Chapter 8's team process fits a founding team; a solo founder can shrink it to a notes doc and a weekly review.

**Bookey's "Critical Thinking" boxes** argue that broad markets (Blue Ocean Strategy) and flexible process (The Lean Startup) can beat narrow focus. That's Bookey's commentary, and the Lean Startup point actually supports the book rather than contradicting it.

**Verdict:** worth applying in full at your stage. Every client, collab or content idea you're testing is cheaper to validate with 10 honest conversations than with a month of building.

## 6. Apply it: Creation100x outreach and your content

Your cold DMs to brokers and small businesses are a Mom Test problem: if the DM pitches services, you'll get polite silence or "looks cool, will let you know" (a zombie lead).

**Slice first.** "Small businesses" is too broad. Pick one who-where pair for 2 weeks, e.g. *real estate brokers in one city who already post property reels on Instagram*. They've shown the behaviour (posting), so they feel the pain.

**DM opener using Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask:**

> Hey \[name\], I'm a student building a small studio that helps brokers get more enquiries from Instagram (vision). Not selling anything right now, just researching (framing). I'm trying to understand why some property reels get leads and others don't (weakness). Your reels are some of the most consistent I've seen in \[city\] (pedestal). Could I ask you 3 quick questions here or on a 10-minute call? (ask)

**The 5 questions to ask (none mention your service):**

1. How do most of your enquiries come in right now?
2. Walk me through the last property you posted on Instagram. Who made the video?
3. What did that reel lead to, any calls or site visits?
4. What have you tried to get more leads from social? What did it cost?
5. Who else in your circle is doing this well? (warm intro)

**Then push for commitment:** if the pain is real, offer one specific next step: "Can I edit your next listing reel free, and if it gets more enquiries we talk about a paid package?" A yes costs them time and access; a "maybe later" tells you it's not their priority.

**Use it in your content too:** before making a reel series, ask 10 followers what they last searched or struggled with, not "would you watch this?". Saves and DMs are commitment; likes are compliments.
